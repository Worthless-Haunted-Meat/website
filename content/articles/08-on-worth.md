# On Worth

*By Ricardo Vega*

---

The name of this organisation is Worthless Haunted Meat. People find it distasteful, which is the point — I'm not the one who assigned that value, I'm just refusing to pretend nobody did.

Because we do have a working definition of human worth, whatever we say at funerals. It's what you provide. Not money exactly, but value to others: labour, care, competence, being useful to someone. We don't state it out loud, and we'd deny it if asked, but watch how quickly a person's standing changes when they stop producing. It's shallow, and I'm not going to defend it. I'm going to take it seriously for a minute, because if that's the real measure, then it has an implication people don't follow through on.

Consider how a person comes to participate in society at all.

You did something, and it worked. Small — you completed a task, someone paid you, someone thanked you, you got picked for the next thing. And that reward taught you the system responds to input. So you did more, and got more, and each reward pointed at the next one until participating became simply what you do. Every part of who you are that looks like character — reliability, ambition, playing by the rules — was built on a long chain of the system paying you back for playing by them.

That's not a moral achievement. It's a feedback loop that happened to close.

Now take a person for whom it didn't. Every time they engaged with the system, it cost them. The job that didn't pay. The lease that was a trap. The agency that lost the file, twice. The programme they qualified for until a rule changed. Not one catastrophe — a hundred small demonstrations that input produces punishment.

They learn exactly what you learned, from the opposite data. The rational response to a system that punishes participation is to stop participating. That isn't a character defect. It's the same learning mechanism that made you dependable, running on a different training set.

And then the thing people miss entirely: they don't stop. They build a parallel one.

Spend any real time near it and you'll find structure everywhere. There's a hierarchy, and everyone in it knows where they stand. There's a pecking order, reciprocity, debts tracked without paper. There is expertise — where water is, which lot the security guard doesn't walk, which church actually opens the door, how to keep a phone charged, who's safe and who isn't, which corner floods. That knowledge is transmitted. It's taught to newcomers. There are people who are good at it and respected for being good at it.

That's resource gathering under constraint, and it is skilled work. The same competence, applied to a warehouse or a supply chain, would read as operational talent. We just don't count it, because we only recognise value that arrives through channels we issue.

So run the shallow definition honestly. If worth is providing value, then a person expertly running a survival operation is providing value — to themselves, and usually to three or four others who depend on their knowledge. It doesn't register as worth because it produces no receipt. The value is real; the accounting is what's missing. And that's a flaw in our ledger, not in them.

Same with the word homeless.

Someone living under a bridge has a home. Not a good one, not one with running water or a door that locks — but a location they return to, defend, improve, and can be found at. It's a dwelling. We call them homeless because the word we actually mean is unhoused, or more honestly, not housed in a way we recognise.

I use the word nomad, and I'll admit it's imperfect. But it carries something the other word doesn't: a nomad has a strategy. Moving where the resources are is what nomads have always done, and it's a rational response to a landscape where resources are scattered and access is unpredictable. Which is exactly the landscape this whole series is about — surplus behind two hundred buildings, no address, no schedule, nothing you could plan around. Of course the response is nomadic. We built the conditions for it.

Now, why does any of this belong in a series about food logistics?

Because who you think you're serving determines what you build.

Believe you're dealing with people who lack worth and need saving, and you build things done to them. Intake forms. Compliance conditions. Programmes that require someone to demonstrate they deserve it. Sites designed so the recipient is passive, which is why so many of them sit empty while people sleep three blocks away — because an offer that costs you your standing is expensive, and people who've been punished by systems can read a system's posture from across a car park.

Believe you're dealing with skilled operators in a hostile resource landscape, and you build something else. You build infrastructure. You don't manage them; you change what's available and let competent people route themselves to it. That's the whole design in the previous pieces — reliable surplus, at a known place, at a known hour. It works precisely because the people it serves are good at resource gathering and will find a reliable node fast. It requires nothing of them but showing up.

And it gives the feedback loop somewhere to start. Not a programme, not a plan — one instance of engaging with the system and having it pay off. A place that's there when it said it would be. That's a single data point against a hundred that say don't bother. It's not redemption and I'm not going to dress it up as such. But the loop that built you had to start somewhere too, and it started small.

The name is a dare, in the end. Say worthless out loud and it stops being able to hide in how we allocate things. Nobody who reads the phrase and flinches quite believes it. What most people believe is something softer and worse: that these are people whose value is currently zero, and might be restored if they'd participate properly.

The value was never zero. It's being generated nightly under conditions most of us wouldn't survive, in a currency our ledger doesn't record.

Fix the ledger.
