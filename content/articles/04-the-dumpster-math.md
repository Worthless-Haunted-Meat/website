# The Dumpster Math

*By Ricardo Vega*

---

Eleven fifteen at night, behind a strip mall you've driven past a thousand times. A shift manager props the back door with a milk crate and carries out four trays of rotisserie chicken, a bakery rack of bread from this morning, and two flats of clamshell salads dated tomorrow. He puts all of it in the dumpster. Total retail, call it four hundred dollars. Total cost to him, nothing — it's already paid for. Total nutritional value at that moment, undiminished. It is, in every sense that matters, food.

Ask him why and he won't say what you expect. He won't say he's worried about being sued. Most managers I've talked to have some vague sense that there's a law protecting them, and they're right — the Bill Emerson Good Samaritan Act has covered good-faith food donation in this country since 1996. Liability isn't the wall.

The wall is that throwing it away is a solved problem and giving it away isn't.

The dumpster takes thirty seconds. It requires no phone call, no receiving party, no paperwork, no decision. Donating requires someone to arrive, in a window, tonight, and it requires him to explain to a district manager why there's a stranger at the back door, and it produces no record that anything of value happened. He is not a bad person. He is a person choosing between a thirty-second solved problem and an unbounded unsolved one at the end of a ten-hour shift.

Now here's the part that changes how you think about it.

That food is not an asset. It's a decaying asset. Its value has a half-life measured in hours. Hot food is worth something for maybe four hours; bakery, a day; those salads, until tomorrow evening. And because value decays with time, and travel costs time, value also decays with distance. Food that's worth four hundred dollars behind that strip mall is worth maybe two hundred fifteen miles away and nothing at all forty miles away, because it doesn't arrive as food anymore. It arrives as garbage that someone now has to dispose of.

This is why the intuitive solution doesn't work. The intuitive solution is centralisation: one big food bank, one warehouse, one truck route, economies of scale. That's how we solve almost every other distribution problem, and it's exactly wrong here. Centralisation trades time for efficiency, and time is the one thing this asset cannot spend. Every mile to the warehouse and every mile back out is value burning off. You end up with a system that handles shelf-stable donations well — canned goods, dry goods, the stuff that doesn't decay — and structurally cannot touch the four hundred dollars in that dumpster. Which is why, despite every food bank in every city doing genuinely good work, that dumpster still gets filled at eleven fifteen tonight.

Perishable surplus has to be routed locally or not at all. Not for sentimental reasons about community. For thermodynamic ones.

And that has a consequence people don't like.

If the routing has to be local, then the routing decision has to be local. You cannot have a city-wide authority deciding where the food from that strip mall goes, because by the time a decision reaches you from downtown, the asset is gone. The decision has to be made inside a radius small enough to beat the decay curve — which means it has to be made by the neighbourhood that dumpster sits in.

That sounds nice until you notice it's also a burden. It means a neighbourhood has to actually decide something. Where does it go? Who picks it up? Where do the people who need it gather, and is that somewhere the neighbourhood is willing to have people gather? Those are real questions with real disagreement behind them, and the moment you ask them out loud you discover the second problem in this series, which is that we have no functioning way to ask a neighbourhood anything.

But hold that thought, because the logistics side has one more piece of good news in it.

Every problem I've described at that back door is an information problem, not a physical one. The food exists. The vehicle exists — somebody in that neighbourhood owns a car with an empty back seat at eleven at night. The need exists, usually within two miles. What doesn't exist is the connective tissue: a way for the manager to signal "there's four trays here, come now," a way for someone to be dispatched, and a record afterward proving it happened.

That last one matters more than it sounds. The record is what turns donation from a favour into a transaction his company can account for. Timestamped handoff, itemised, logged. Now the district manager isn't asking why there's a stranger at the back door — he's looking at a monthly report showing eleven hundred pounds diverted from waste disposal, with the disposal cost saved sitting right next to it. The paperwork burden that killed donation becomes the thing that justifies it, and it costs the manager nothing, because the app generates it as a by-product of the pickup.

That's the whole logistics thesis. Surplus is perishable, so routing is local, so deciding is local — and the technology's job is not to move food. It's to make the signal, the dispatch, and the receipt cheap enough that the thirty-second dumpster stops being the easier option.

Which leaves the question I dodged twice in this piece. Local decision by whom, exactly? Because if your answer is "the people who show up to community meetings," I have some numbers for you that are going to ruin your week.
