# One Person, One Problem

*By Ricardo Vega*

---

My friend Mr. M is a taxi driver, and in a few years he probably won't be.

I'm not going to argue about whether that's good or bad. Both sides of that argument are boring and neither changes what happens to him. Autonomous vehicles will take that work or they won't, on a timeline nobody can predict, and moralising about it does nothing for a man with a car payment. What interests me is the question underneath: when the thing you did for money stops existing, what's left?

In Mr. M's case, quite a lot. He coaches his kid's soccer team. He's good at it — not in the way every parent thinks they're good at it, actually good, the kind of good where other parents ask which team their child can get onto. He does it for free, on Saturdays, after driving all week.

So here's the whole argument of this piece in one line: everybody loves something, and almost nobody has ever been able to make a living from it, and the reason isn't talent — it's overhead.

Think about what stands between Mr. M and being paid to coach. Not skill. He has that. It's registration and insurance and a business structure. Scheduling and payments and cancellations. Marketing to people who don't know he exists. A website. Handling parents. Liability waivers. Tax filings. Every one of those is a task he's never done, would hate, and would be bad at — and any one of them is enough to stop the whole thing before it starts.

That's the overhead that has always separated a skill from a livelihood. It's why the world is full of people who are genuinely excellent at something and do it for free on weekends.

That overhead just collapsed. Quietly, in about eighteen months, and mostly for people who already knew how to build things.

Everything on that list is now a conversation with a language model and an afternoon. Not a metaphor — an actual afternoon. The registration research, the scheduling system, the payment link, the waiver, the site, the follow-up emails. The work that used to require either money he doesn't have or knowledge he doesn't have now requires neither.

But — and this is the part I keep running into — he doesn't know that. And knowing it isn't the same as being able to do it. There's a gap between the tools existing and a taxi driver using them, and that gap is entirely populated by people like me.

That's what this arm of the nonprofit is for, and it has exactly one rule.

Find one person. Solve one problem. Nothing more.

Not a platform for displaced workers. Not a programme with cohorts and outcomes and a dashboard. One person, one problem, all the way to done. Because the thing that kills every good intention in this space is scope — you start with a taxi driver and a soccer team and end up designing a system for forty thousand hypothetical people you've never met, and then you build nothing.

One person is achievable this month. And one person actually earning from the thing they love is worth more than a strategy document about a thousand.

Now, why does this belong in a nonprofit about homelessness and food?

Because it's the same failure, further upstream. This whole series is about the catching function — the thing small towns did for free, that cities stopped doing, that nobody was ever assigned. A man loses his work, and the loop that connected his effort to a reward breaks, and there's no department whose job it is to notice.

But he didn't lose his usefulness when he lost his job. Mr. M is exactly as good at coaching the day after driving stops paying as the day before. What he lost was the only channel he had for converting what he can do into what he needs. Catching him doesn't mean finding him a shelter. It means finding the second channel before he needs the first.

And there's a version of this I want to name honestly, because it applies to me. The people who can do this — who can sit with someone for an hour, work out what they're actually good at, and then build the scaffolding around it in a weekend — are standing on an enormous gift we did nothing to earn. Twenty years ago, giving Mr. M a functioning business would have been a six-month project and a real budget. Now it's a Saturday, because a language model does the parts I'm not expert in and I do the parts of knowing what to ask for.

That's the shoulders of giants, handed to us for the price of a subscription. And what most people are doing with it is shipping features slightly faster at work.

So the ask is the same as the last piece, aimed at the same people. If you can build things, you can convert somebody's Saturday hobby into a Saturday income, and it will cost you one weekend and a few conversations. Not a charity. Not a course. One person you already know, whose thing you already admire, who has never once considered that anyone would pay for it.

And it doesn't need to wait until the job disappears. That's the point of doing it now. If the second channel is already running when the first one stops, there's no fall to catch.

One person. One problem. Then the next one.
